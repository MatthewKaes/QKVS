At dawn the research vessel crossed the outer edge of the bay. The sea was calm, but a low bank of fog hid the channel markers and softened every sound from shore. Mara checked the navigation display, compared it with the paper chart, and wrote the time beside the first waypoint. The crew had learned that a digital record was useful only when someone could explain how it had been produced.

The morning task was to survey a chain of temperature sensors anchored at different depths. Each instrument stored a week of readings and transmitted a short status message when the vessel approached. Some messages arrived immediately. Others were delayed by waves, weak batteries, or the angle of the antenna. The order of reception therefore differed from the order in which the measurements had been taken.

Jon prepared the sampling log. For every sensor he recorded its identifier, depth, calibration date, battery voltage, and the exact interval covered by the downloaded data. He also noted failed contacts instead of silently omitting them. A missing observation could be harmless, or it could mark the most important event of the trip. The distinction depended on evidence that would only become visible later.

Near the third station, the surface water warmed while the deeper probes remained cold. The pattern resembled a tidal front observed during the previous season. Mara searched the earlier notes and found that the old front had moved north after a change in wind. That historical passage did not answer the present question by itself, but it identified which measurements deserved closer comparison.

By noon the fog lifted. The team could see the lighthouse, two cargo ships, and a line of dark water where currents met. They repeated the shallow cast to separate a genuine temperature change from a sensor fault. The second cast agreed with the first within the calibration tolerance. Jon marked the result as confirmed and kept both raw records so another analyst could reproduce the conclusion.

The vessel then turned toward deeper water. Several sensors reported ordinary conditions, and their messages were brief. One sensor reported a sudden salinity decrease followed by a slow recovery. The crew compared that sequence with rainfall records, river discharge, and the timing of the tide. No single source was decisive. Together they supported a modest explanation: fresh water had crossed the station shortly before the vessel arrived.

During the return trip, Mara reviewed the log for accidental shortcuts. Station names could reveal location, timestamps could reveal tide, and message length could reveal whether an instrument had raised an alarm. Any predictive analysis would need to distinguish useful historical context from labels hidden in the input format. She added a note requiring future tests to preserve plausible alternatives while changing only the evidence under examination.

Jon summarized the day without turning the observations into a stronger claim than they supported. The survey had found a moving boundary, one likely fresh-water pulse, and several ordinary profiles. It had not established a long-term trend. The instruments had behaved consistently, but the short trip could not prove that they would remain reliable throughout the season.

Back at the dock, the team copied the raw files to two independent stores and verified their checksums. The written log was scanned, indexed, and linked to the corresponding sensor packets. A small manifest recorded software versions and the transformation applied to every exported table. The archive was deliberately plain. Its purpose was not to impress a reader, but to make disagreement and correction possible.

The next morning another analyst opened the archive without speaking to the crew. She reconstructed the route, matched the repeated casts, and recovered the same ordering ambiguity in the radio messages. Her result did not prove that every interpretation was correct. It did show that the evidence, assumptions, and missing observations were sufficiently explicit for an independent review.

This distinction guided the next experiment. A model would read a continuous record and decide which older passages to revisit under a fixed budget. Recent context would always remain available. Historical access would be counted by actual occurrence, including repeated or overlapping reads. Training could inspect dense reference information, but deployed selection would have to produce bounded addresses directly from causal state.

The evaluation would use later, nonoverlapping passages from the same record. Training text would never appear in the held-out streams. Rolling attention, fixed offsets, an untrained selector, and the trained selector would share the frozen reader, token boundaries, scoring rule, and access budget. Any difference would be reported as a measured property of this small study, not as proof of a general architecture.
